YOUR SPOKEN WALKTHROUGH

What every dashboard widget means

Follow these cards from left to right and top to bottom. “Say this” is deliberately non-technical and clinically cautious.

01

Choose an ECG

Widget: Prepared example loads MIT-BIH record 100. Upload accepts EDF, WFDB or numeric CSV. The source card confirms the file, channel count, sampling rate and duration.

“I begin by choosing the ECG. For this demonstration I use the prepared record, but the same pipeline accepts our study formats.”
02

Segment controls

Widget: Channel chooses the lead. Start and Duration choose the analyzed time interval. CSV sampling rate appears only when it cannot be inferred.

“Here I choose exactly which lead and time segment enter the analysis, so the input is explicit and reproducible.”
03

Timestamp track and calibration

Widget: NeuroKit/Zhai selects the displayed R-peak track; it does not declare a clinical winner. Calibration must be at least five because Varon mathematically stacks five aligned QRS complexes.

“I can inspect either independent detector track. At least five early eligible beats define a patient-specific shape reference, which is then frozen.”
04

Workflow ribbon

Widget: Signal → R peaks → four parallel branches → matrix. A colored step is active or complete; a pale step is still waiting for enough history.

“This ribbon is the map: one ECG supplies shared heartbeat anchors, four branches measure different properties, and the matrix aligns their outputs in time.”
05

Slow-motion narrator

Widget: NOW, Step, title and Why update with the cursor. The seven dots summarize overall progress.

“The narrator explains what becomes available at this instant and why a feature may still be blank.”
06

Live ECG and playback

Widget: Blue markers are detected R peaks. Shaded bands show the frozen patient crop and automatic QRS boundaries. Amber is the extraction cursor. Previous/next beat, play, timeline and speed control the replay.

“The waveform is never disconnected from the numbers. I can pause, move one beat at a time, or replay as slowly as one-quarter speed.”
07

Morphology: calibration

Widget: Collected counts eligible calibration beats. P95 onset→R and R→offset estimate asymmetric crop extents. Applied crop and freeze time show the fixed patient/lead window.

“Instead of forcing one crop on every patient, the algorithm learns separate left and right QRS extents from early beats, then stops adapting.”
08

Morphology: three shape lanes

Widget: Varon shows five-QRS λ1–λ5 shape-energy features. Template shows similarity and D-DTW to the frozen whole-cycle reference. P–QRS–T shows interpretable QRS, PR, QT and polarity measurements.

“These lanes answer three questions: how a five-beat QRS group changes, how a beat differs from the patient template, and which specific waveform intervals changed.”
09

RR / HRV

Widget: Heart rate and RR update beat by beat. SD1/SD2 describe Poincaré variability; CSI/ModCSI and J1/J2 combine geometry with heart-rate trend. The plot shows recent RR values.

“Morphology describes shape; this purple branch describes rhythm timing and how that timing evolves.”
10

PRSA / BPRSA

Widget: The 80-beat counter must fill first. Mean RR/SDNN summarize timing; PRSA slopes describe acceleration-linked RR behavior; BPRSA slopes use R amplitude as a respiration-related trigger. Anchor and support fields audit computability.

“This is autonomic and cardiorespiratory context from an 80-beat history. It is a feature extractor, not the paper’s seizure classifier.”
11

Signal quality

Widget: Each causal 10-second window reports finite/flat fractions, spectral basSQI/pSQI, detector-agreement bSQI/qSQI, RR support and beat-template correlation. Computable reports how many prerequisites were available.

“This branch measures evidence about signal quality. It deliberately does not turn those measurements into a clean-versus-artifact label.”
12

Conduction and repolarization

Widget: PR, QRS, QT/JT and Tpeak→Tend come from automatic landmarks. Three QTc formulas are shown side by side. ΔPR/ΔQT show beat-to-beat change; 5-, 9- and 30-beat cards add causal context.

“These are transparent timing measurements. They are information-only because automatic landmark validation is still pending; no abnormality is assigned.”
13

Combined feature matrix

Widget: Each row is one time-aligned snapshot. Columns can be filtered by branch. Complete rows satisfy the core morphology/HRV gate. Cell color is relative within each column. Export CSV saves the measurements and safety flags.

“Combined means causally time-aligned—not averaged and not classified. The heat colors help us see change; they are not a risk score.”
14

Status pills and safety language

Widget: Waiting/Building/Complete describe computational state, not health. Measurements only, no classifier, no artifact label and information-only labels define the permitted interpretation.

“Everything on this screen is an auditable intermediate measurement. The demo does not diagnose a seizure, artifact, or conduction disorder.”
15

How to read the live ECG graph

Axes: horizontal is time in seconds; vertical is raw lead amplitude. Blue dots mark detected R peaks, teal marks the automatic QRS boundary, blue shading is the frozen patient crop and amber is the current replay time. A large deflection can be physiology or noise; this graph alone cannot decide which.

“This is the source waveform. Every colored marker shows exactly where the later measurements came from.”
16

How to read the five aligned QRS graph

Axes: horizontal is milliseconds from R = 0; vertical is raw lead amplitude. The five overlaid traces are the exact QRS crops used in one Varon window. Tight overlap means similar shape; separation means shape or amplitude changed, or noise/boundary error entered the crop. λ1–λ5 summarize these traces and are scale-dependent—not diagnoses.

“I align five beats on the R peak. The picture shows their actual overlap; the five eigenvalues turn that same picture into numeric columns.”
17

How to read the template graph

Axes: horizontal is normalized cardiac-cycle phase around R; vertical is centered lead amplitude. Green traces are the frozen patient reference and blue is the current cycle. Correlation nearer 1 means more similar shape; normalized RMSE, residual and D-DTW are better read as smaller = closer.

“This lane asks how far the current whole beat has moved from this patient’s own early reference.”
18

How to read the P–QRS–T graph

Axes: horizontal is milliseconds from R; vertical is ECG amplitude. Dots label automatic P, QRS and T landmarks and the shaded band marks QRS. Horizontal distances become PR, QRS, QT, JT and ST intervals; vertical distances become amplitudes. Missing dots mean unavailable landmarks.

“This is the interpretable lane: the software shows the landmarks before it subtracts their times or amplitudes.”
19

How to read RR / HRV and PRSA graphs

RR graph: time versus milliseconds between R peaks; a higher point means a longer interval and slower instantaneous rate. Purple is the causal median and gray means unsupported. PRSA/BPRSA: time around an anchor versus averaged RR; the center slope summarizes acceleration/deceleration-linked timing, while BPRSA anchors on R amplitude.

“These graphs describe when beats occur and how timing changes; they do not describe waveform shape.”
20

How to read the signal-quality graph

Axes: horizontal is the end time of each causal 10-second window; vertical is a unitless 0–1 measurement. Each colored line is one available SQI. A value being plotted means it was computable, not that the signal passed a clean/noisy threshold.

“The quality branch exposes its evidence and availability; it intentionally does not assign an artifact label.”
21

How to read the conduction graph

Axes: horizontal is beat time; vertical is interval duration in milliseconds. Separate lines track PR, QRS, QT and JT. Look for stability or change across beats, but keep the automatic-landmark caveat visible: this is information-only until clinically validated.

“The line heights are transparent interval measurements, not normal-versus-abnormal decisions.”
22

How to read the matrix heat colors

Rows: time-aligned beats or windows. Columns: extracted measurements. Color is normalized separately inside each column, so darker means relatively high for that feature—not high clinical risk—and colors cannot be compared across differently scaled columns.

“The matrix is where the branches meet in time. Its colors reveal within-feature change; they are not a classifier score.”
